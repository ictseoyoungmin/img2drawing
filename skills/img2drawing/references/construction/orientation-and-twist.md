# Orientation and twist

Use this leaf when the drawing has roughly plausible masses but still feels flatter, more frontal,
more symmetric, or less twisted than the observed subject.

This guide owns the **spatial orientation of the major masses and their relation to one another**.
It does not own final contour, anatomy detail, or value rendering.

## Read each major mass as an oriented form

For the head, ribcage, and pelvis, ask:

- what is the screen-space tilt of the mass;
- which way is it turning relative to the viewer;
- which side or plane is nearer and which is farther;
- how much of the front / side / top plane is exposed;
- where the projected centerline or cross-axis travels;
- where the visible width expands or compresses;
- which edges disappear, overlap, or reappear because of the turn.

Do not infer a hidden plane merely to complete a textbook solid. Use only the visible evidence.
A centerline or cross-axis is a reasoning mark, not a decorative construction symbol.

## Compare the masses, not just the parts

The pose depends on **differences between orientations**:

- head turn against ribcage turn;
- ribcage turn against pelvis turn;
- shoulder axis against pelvis axis;
- near shoulder against far shoulder;
- near hip against far hip;
- neck exit point against the ribcage orientation;
- compression on one side of the torso against stretch on the other;
- prop depth/overlap against the body planes it crosses.

Two individually plausible masses can still make the whole pose wrong if their relative rotation
is lost. Do not normalize the figure toward a comfortable frontal or symmetric solution.

## Structural read before local description

Before spending marks on local contour, facial accents, clothing detail, or broad value, the
current drawing should make these relationships readable without relying on tone:

- head / ribcage / pelvis orientation;
- shoulder / pelvis counter-relation;
- support and weight tendency;
- major limb anchor chains;
- stance and large negative spaces;
- major prop axis and body overlap when present.

This is not a runtime stage or irreversible gate. It is a drawing decision rule. If a later
inspection disproves one of these relations, return here immediately and revise the responsible
construction marks.

## Common flattening failures

Re-observe when any of these occur:

- the reference is clearly turned but the drawing reads front-facing;
- near/far shoulders or hips become equally prominent;
- head, ribcage, and pelvis axes drift toward parallel alignment;
- both sides of a garment or torso become mechanically symmetric;
- limbs become parallel rails because their parent mass orientation was lost;
- a prop appears pasted on top of the body instead of passing through a believable depth order;
- local contours look clean while the whole pose has lost the subject's twist.

Do not repair these failures by adding darker tone, more folds, more facial detail, or extra
contour lines. The responsible relation is upstream.

## Measurement boundary

Prefer comparative statements such as "ribcage turns farther away than pelvis" or "subject-right
shoulder is nearer" over invented numeric angles. Use measurement only when a visible projected
axis or landmark relation can actually support it.

When the orientation itself is uncertain in the reference, return to
`../observation/visual-observation.md`. When the mass orientation is credible but projected limb
spacing/depth is still wrong, route to `foreshortening-and-depth.md`.
